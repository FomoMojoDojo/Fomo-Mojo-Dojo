What it is. Your strategy expressed as a set of hard, connected choices: what you're aiming for, where you've chosen to play, how you'll win there, what that win will take, and what keeps it going. Each choice sets up the next — that's what makes it a strategy rather than a list of goals.

Why it matters. Most "strategies" are really an aspiration plus a budget. Real strategy is the set of trade-offs that actually create an advantage — and deciding where you won't play matters just as much as where you will. Once the choices link up, you can finally tell whether the things you do every day are pushing the strategy forward or quietly pulling against it. The point isn't to fill in a framework; it's to see clearly whether your choices hold together.

When it's blurry, the problem usually isn't your product — it's that the line connecting your customer's needs, your strategy, and your value hasn't been drawn sharply enough yet.

What it is. The distinct paths you could take toward your strategy — each a coherent direction with its own logic, not a to-do list. Each route is framed as a choice: what you'd be betting on, and what would have to be true for it to work.

Why it matters. Jumping straight to a single plan hides the fact that a choice was even made. Laying out real routes makes the decision visible and reversible — you can see what each path bets on before you commit, and you keep the options you set aside as context instead of losing them. Keeping meaningful alternatives open until the evidence earns the commitment is how you avoid betting the year on a guess.

How it connects. Routes carry your strategy into action, and each one leans on particular opportunities and assumptions about your customers. They're where the choices in your strategy meet the realities in your needs and evidence.

How to use it. For each route, read the "what would have to be true" — that's where the risk lives. Often the strongest next move isn't picking a route at all, but cheaply testing the one assumption that most separates two of them. A route with no alternative it's been chosen over, or no conditions attached, isn't ready yet.

Where this comes from. Early routes are generated from outside-in evidence as directions worth exploring — candidates, not commitments. They sharpen as your own evidence shows which assumptions actually hold.
